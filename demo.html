1. What is a Ghazal?

The ghazal (غزل) is one of the most celebrated poetic forms in Urdu, Persian, Arabic, and Turkish literary traditions. The word "ghazal" derives from the Arabic root meaning "to talk to women" or "the cry of a wounded deer" — both definitions hint at its deeply emotional, often love-soaked nature.

A ghazal is a collection of autonomous couplets (called ashʿār, singular sher) that are bound together by a strict rhyme scheme and meter, yet each couplet stands independently in meaning. This thematic independence is what distinguishes the ghazal from a nazm (a poem with a continuous narrative).

The ghazal tradition stretches back over a thousand years. It flourished in 7th-century Arabic poetry, was refined in Persian by masters like Hafiz Shirazi and Rumi, and reached its zenith in Urdu through poets like Mir Taqi Mir, Mirza Ghalib, and Faiz Ahmed Faiz. In the 20th century, poets like Agha Shahid Ali brought the form into English literature.

Hazāroñ ḳhwāhisheñ aisī ki har ḳhwāhish pe dam nikle
Bahut nikle mere armān lekin phir bhī kam nikle — Mirza Ghalib
Translation: There are a thousand desires such that each desire takes my breath away / Many of my wishes came true, but they still felt too few.
This is the matla (opening couplet) of one of Ghalib's most famous ghazals. Notice how both lines end with "kam nikle" — this establishes the radif (refrain) and qafiya (rhyme) for the entire ghazal.

2. Anatomy of a Ghazal

Every ghazal is built from the following structural components:

2.1 Sher (شعر) — The Couplet

The Sher is the fundamental unit of a ghazal. Each sher consists of exactly two lines called misra (مصرع):

  • Misra-e-Ula (مصرعِ اُولیٰ) — The first line of the couplet
  • Misra-e-Sani (مصرعِ ثانی) — The second line of the couplet

Each sher must be self-contained — it must express a complete thought independently. A reader should be able to understand any sher without reading the rest of the ghazal. This is the hallmark of the ghazal form.

2.2 Matla (مطلع) — The Opening Couplet

The Matla is the first sher of the ghazal. It is special because both lines end with the qafiya (rhyme) and radif (refrain). The matla establishes the rhyme pattern and meter that all subsequent couplets must follow.

A ghazal may have a Husn-e-Matla (حُسنِ مطلع) — a second matla immediately after the first one. This is considered a mark of the poet's skill.

2.3 Maqta (مقطع) — The Closing Couplet

The Maqta is the final couplet of the ghazal. By convention, the poet includes their takhallus (تخلّص — pen name) in this couplet. This serves as a signature.

Huī muddat ki 'Ghālib' mar gayā par yād aatā hai
Woh har ek baat par kahnā ki yūñ hotā to kyā hotā — Mirza Ghalib (Maqta — note the pen name 'Ghalib' in the first line)

2.4 Beyt / Ashʿār (ابیات / اشعار) — Interior Couplets

All couplets between the matla and maqta are simply called ashʿār (plural of sher). Only the second line (misra-e-sani) of each interior sher must end with the qafiya and radif.

3. Rhyme Scheme — Qafiya and Radif

The ghazal's distinctive music comes from two interlocking rhyme elements:

3.1 Qafiya (قافیہ) — The Rhyme

The Qafiya is the rhyming word or syllable that appears immediately before the radif. All qafiya words in a ghazal must share the same rhyme pattern — they must end with the same consonant-vowel combination.

Example: If the qafiya words are jamāl, kamāl, ḳhayāl, visāl — they all share the -āl sound.

3.2 Radif (ردیف) — The Refrain

The Radif is the exact word or phrase that repeats unchanged after the qafiya at the end of every relevant line. In the matla, both lines carry the radif. In all subsequent shers, only the second line carries it.

A ghazal can be Ba-radif (with refrain) or Ghair-muraddaf (غیر مردّف — without refrain), where only the qafiya provides the end-rhyme.

3.3 Rhyme Scheme Formula

The formal rhyme scheme of a ghazal is: AA BA CA DA EA ...

Couplet Misra-e-Ula (Line 1) Misra-e-Sani (Line 2)
Matla (1st) Ends with qafiya + radif Ends with qafiya + radif
Sher 2 Free ending Ends with qafiya + radif

3.4 Worked Example — Identifying Qafiya and Radif

Dil-e-nādāñ tujhe huā kyā hai
Āḳhir is dard kī davā kyā hai — Mirza Ghalib
Radif: "kyā hai" (repeats exactly at the end of both lines)
Qafiya: "huā" and "davā" — both end in the -ā sound before "kyā hai"
Rhyme scheme: Every subsequent sher's second line will end: [word ending in -ā] + "kyā hai"

Continuing this ghazal:

Hum haiñ mushtāq aur woh bezār
Yā ilāhī yeh mājarā kyā hai
Note: In the interior couplet, only the second line ends with the qafiya (-ā in "mājarā") + radif ("kyā hai"). The first line is free to end however the poet chooses.

4. Meter — Beher and the Aruuz System

The Beher (بحر) is the metrical pattern that governs every line of a ghazal. The science of Urdu/Persian meter is called Aruuz (عروض), adapted from Arabic prosody.

4.1 Syllable Types

Urdu meter is based on the alternation of long and short syllables:

Short (harka) ∪ (or 1) 1 beat ka, na, di, su
Long (sakin) — (or 2) 2 beats kā, nā, dil, rāt

4.4 Key Rules of Meter

  • Every misra (line) of every sher in the ghazal must follow the same beher exactly.
  • Poets may use Zihāf (زحاف) — permitted metrical variations where a long syllable is shortened or a short one is lengthened at specific positions.
  • The meter creates the musical rhythm (laye) when the ghazal is recited or sung.
  • To check meter, read the line aloud and clap on long syllables — the rhythm should be consistent throughout.

5. Point of View in Ghazals

Understanding point of view (POV) is essential for ghazal writing. Because each sher is thematically independent, a single ghazal can shift between different perspectives across couplets.

5.1 First Person (متکلّم — Mutakallim)

The most common POV in ghazals. The poet speaks as "I" (maiñ / main / ham), expressing personal emotions — longing, heartbreak, ecstasy, philosophical reflection. The first person creates intimacy between the poet and the reader/listener.

Main ne majnūñ pe laṛakpan meñ Asad
Bahut sahī tere kahne lagā

Better example:
Ranj se ḳhūgar huā insāñ to miṭ jātā hai ranj
Mushkileñ mujh par paṛīñ itnī ki āsāñ ho gaʾīñ — Mirza Ghalib (First Person — "mujh par" = upon me)
First person markers in Urdu: maiñ (I), ham (we/I — majestic singular), mujhe/mujh ko (to me), merā/merī/mere (my/mine), hamāra (our).

5.2 Second Person (مخاطب — Mukhātab)

The poet directly addresses the beloved (mehboob), God, or an abstract concept using "you" (tū, tum, āp). This creates a sense of conversation or pleading. In classical ghazals, the beloved is usually addressed in second person.

Tū kisi rail sī guzartī hai
Main kisi pul sā thartharātā hūñ — Jaun Elia (Second Person — "Tū" = You)
Second person markers in Urdu: tū (informal you), tum (you), āp (formal you), tujhe/tumheñ (to you), terā/terī/tere (your — informal), tumhārā (your).

5.3 Third Person (غائب — Ghāʾib)

Less common but powerful — the poet speaks about the beloved or a situation in the third person (woh/wuh — he/she/that). This creates a sense of distance, observation, or storytelling. "Woh" in Urdu ghazals is gender-neutral, adding a deliberate ambiguity.

Woh āye hamāre ghar ḳhudā kī qudrat
Kabhī ham ko un kī, kabhī apne ghar kī yaad āʾī — Mirza Ghalib (Third Person for beloved — "Woh" = They/He/She)
Third person markers in Urdu: woh/wuh (he/she/they — gender neutral), us/un (him/her/them), uskā/uskī/uske (his/her/their).

Key point: The gender-neutral "woh" allows ghazals to transcend earthly love — the beloved can be a person, God (in Sufi tradition), or an abstract ideal.

5.4 POV Shifts Within a Ghazal

Since each sher is an independent unit, a ghazal frequently shifts POV between couplets. This is not only acceptable but expected. A typical ghazal might:

  • Begin with first person reflection in the matla
  • Shift to second person (addressing the beloved) in the next sher
  • Move to third person observation
  • Return to first person in the maqta (with the poet's name)
POV Pronouns Common Usage Emotional Effect
First Person maiñ, ham, mujhe, merā Personal feelings, self-reflection, pain, joy Intimacy, vulnerability, confession
Second Person tū, tum, āp, tujhe, terā Addressing beloved, complaints, pleas, admiration Direct engagement, confrontation, longing
Third Person woh, us, uskā, un Describing beloved from afar, philosophical observation Distance, mystery, universality

6. Grammar of Ghazal Writing

Ghazal grammar draws from Urdu, Persian (Farsi), and Arabic. Understanding the grammatical conventions is crucial for composing technically sound ghazals.

6.1 Izāfat (اضافت) — The Possessive Construction

The Izāfat is a Persian grammatical construction using a short -e (zer) sound to link two words in a possessive or descriptive relationship. It is one of the most distinctive features of ghazal language.

  • Dil-e-nādāñ = Heart + of + naive = The naive heart
  • Shām-e-ġharībāñ = Evening + of + strangers = The evening of strangers
  • Āb-e-ḥayāt = Water + of + life = The water of life
  • Bād-e-ṣabā = Wind + of + morning = The morning breeze

6.2 Verb Forms and Tenses

Ghazals use a rich range of Urdu verb forms:

TenseExampleUsage in Ghazal
Simple Present (Hāl-e-Muṭlaq) aatā hai (comes) Universal truths, habitual actions
Present Continuous aa rahā hai (is coming) Immediate action, vivid imagery
Simple Past (Māzī-e-Muṭlaq) aayā (came) Narrative, recollection
Subjunctive (Ihtimāli) aaye (may come) Wishes, possibilities, prayers
Imperative (Amr) aa (come!), aaiye (please come) Commands, invitations, pleas to beloved
Conditional (Sharti) agar aaye to... (if comes, then...) Hypothetical scenarios, "what if" couplets

6.3 Poetic Licenses (Zarūrat-e-Sher)

Zarūrat-e-Sher (ضرورتِ شعر) refers to grammatical liberties taken for the sake of meter and rhyme:

  • Word contraction: "karā" instead of "kiyā" (did)
  • Vowel lengthening/shortening: adjusting syllable length for meter
  • Archaic forms: using classical forms of words (e.g., "ḳhwāb" for "ḳhāb")
  • Persian/Arabic plurals: "ashk" → "ashkān" (tears), "dard" → "dard-hā"

Important: While poetic license is accepted, excessive grammatical distortion is considered a flaw (ʿaib). The best poets maintain natural language flow.

6.4 Gender in Urdu Ghazal Grammar

Urdu has grammatical gender (masculine/feminine) that affects verb conjugation, adjective agreement, and postpositions. In ghazals:

  • The beloved ("mehboob") is traditionally referred to with masculine grammar even when the beloved may be female — a Persian literary convention.
  • "Woh" (he/she/they) is deliberately gender-ambiguous.
  • Modern ghazal poets sometimes break this convention to explicitly use feminine forms.

6.5 Sentence Structure

Urdu follows SOV (Subject-Object-Verb) word order, but in poetry, this is flexible:

  • Normal: Main tum se mohabbat kartā hūñ (I love you)
  • Poetic inversion: Mohabbat kartā hūñ tum se main (Love, I do, for you)

Such inversions are used to place the rhyming word at the end or to create emphasis.

8. Rules and Conventions of Ghazal Writing

8.1 Mandatory Rules

  1. Minimum 5 couplets — A ghazal must have at least 5 shers (traditionally 5–15, sometimes up to 25).
  2. Consistent meter (beher) — Every misra in the ghazal must follow the same metrical pattern.
  3. Matla requirement — The first couplet must have both lines ending with qafiya + radif.
  4. Qafiya consistency — All rhyming words must share the same rhyme pattern throughout the ghazal.
  5. Thematic independence — Each sher must be a self-contained unit of meaning.
  6. Radif consistency — If the ghazal has a radif, it must repeat unchanged in every applicable line.

8.2 Strong Conventions (Not Mandatory but Expected)

  1. Takhallus in maqta — The poet's pen name should appear in the final couplet.
  2. Thematic diversity — While love is the primary theme, a good ghazal touches on multiple aspects — philosophy, nature, social commentary.
  3. Each sher should shine independently — A single memorable sher can make a ghazal immortal.
  4. Natural language — Avoid excessive contortion of grammar for the sake of rhyme.

8.3 Common Flaws (Uyūb) to Avoid

  • Iʿāda — Repeating the same qafiya word in two different shers (a serious flaw).
  • Riyāʿat-e-laf̤z̤ī — Forced or unnatural phrasing just to accommodate meter or rhyme.
  • Tautology — Repeating the same idea in both lines of a sher without adding depth.
  • Weak maqta — A maqta that feels forced or where the pen name is awkwardly inserted.
  • Broken meter — Inconsistent syllable counts that disrupt the beher.

8.4 The Art of the Sher

The best shers achieve the following qualities:

  • Maʿnī-āfarīnī (meaning-creation) — Layered meaning that reveals itself on multiple readings.
  • Surprise (Wardat) — The second line should add an unexpected twist to the first.
  • Economy of language — Saying the most with the fewest words.
  • Universal resonance — Personal emotion that speaks to everyone.
  • Musical beauty — The sound of the words should be pleasing even to someone who doesn't understand the language.

9. Annotated Ghazal Examples

9.1 Mirza Ghalib — "Hazāroñ ḳhwāhisheñ aisī"

Complete Ghazal with Analysis

Matla
Hazāroñ ḳhwāhisheñ aisī ki har ḳhwāhish pe dam nikle
Bahut nikle mere armān lekin phir bhī kam nikle
Sher 2
Dare kyūñ merā qātil kyā rahegā uskī gardan par
Woh ḳhūñ jo chashm-e-tar se ʿumr bhar yūñ dam-ba-dam nikle
Sher 3
Nikalnā ḳhuld se Ādam kā sunte āʾe haiñ lekin
Bahut be-ābru ho kar tere kūche se ham nikle
Sher 4
Bharam khul jāʾe zālim tere qāmat kī darāzī kā
Agar is turra-e-pur-pech kā pechān-o-ḳham nikle
Sher 5
Magar likhwāʾe koʾī us ko ḳhaṭ to ham se likhwāʾe
Ki uskī ḳhush-ḳhaṭī se harf-e-haq bhī be-num nikle
Maqta
Huī muddat ki 'Ghālib' mar gayā par yād aatā hai
Woh har ek baat par kahnā ki yūñ hotā to kyā hotā
Technical Analysis:
Radif: "nikle" (came out / emerged)
Qafiya: dam, kam, dam-ba-dam, ham, ḳham, num — all ending in -am sound
Beher: Beher-e-Hazaj Musaddas Mahzuf (a variation of Hazaj meter)
POV: Primarily first person ("mere armān" = my desires, "ham nikle" = we emerged) with shifts to third person ("uskī gardan" = on his/her neck)
Takhallus: "Ghalib" appears in the maqta

Note on the Maqta: The final couplet brilliantly breaks from the "nikle" radif pattern — this is a famous feature of this particular ghazal where Ghalib appends additional couplets with a different radif, a practice some scholars debate.

9.2 Mir Taqi Mir — "Dīkhā to dil meñ"

Matla
Dīkhā to dil meñ jagah hī nahīñ
Is tīsha-e-vahshat ko samjhā hī nahīñ
Sher 2
Ulṭī ho gaʾīñ sab tadbīreñ kuchh na davā ne kaam kiyā
Dekha is bīmārī-e-dil ne āḳhir kaam-e-masīḥā hī nahīñ
Mir's Style: Mir Taqi Mir, known as the "God of Urdu Poetry" (Ḳhudā-e-Suḳhan), used simpler, more conversational Urdu compared to Ghalib. His ghazals are marked by an intense sincerity of emotion and a deceptive simplicity that hides profound depth.

9.3 Faiz Ahmed Faiz — "Mujh se Pahlī sī Mohabbat"

Matla
Mujh se pahlī sī mohabbat merī mehboob na māñg
Maiñ ne samjhā thā ki tū hai to darāḳhshāñ hai hayāt
Terā gham gar na hotā, gham-e-daurān gar na hotā
Chale bhī āʾo ki gulshan kā kārobār chale
Faiz's Contribution: Faiz Ahmed Faiz revolutionized the ghazal by introducing social and political themes alongside traditional love poetry. In his work, the "beloved" often becomes a metaphor for revolution, freedom, or the homeland. He blended progressive realism with classical beauty, creating a new dimension in ghazal writing. His POV frequently shifts between personal love (first/second person) and social commentary (third person/observational).

9.4 Ahmad Faraz — "Ranjish hī sahī"

Ranjish hī sahī, dil hī dukhāne ke liye aa
Aa phir se mujhe chhoṛ ke jaane ke liye aa — Ahmad Faraz (Matla)
Analysis:
Radif: "ke liye aa" (come for the sake of...)
Qafiya: "dukhāne" and "jaane" — ending in -āne
POV: First person speaker addressing the beloved in second person ("aa" = come, imperative mood). The matla demonstrates the classic ghazal paradox — the lover invites pain and heartbreak just to see the beloved once more.
Grammar note: "Ranjish hī sahī" uses the subjunctive/concessive mood — "even if it be displeasure" — a sophisticated grammatical construction.

9.5 Jaun Elia — Modern Ghazal

Bahut dinoñ meñ mohabbat ko yeh haal hotā
Agar tū rab hai mere, to terā sawāl hotā

Tū kisi rail sī guzartī hai
Main kisi pul sā thartharātā hūñ — Jaun Elia
Jaun Elia's Style: Known for his raw, unfiltered emotional expression. Jaun broke many conventions while maintaining the technical framework. His ghazals feature modern imagery (trains, bridges) alongside classical structures. The second sher here uses a striking modern simile — comparing the beloved to a train and the lover to a trembling bridge.

9.6 Parveen Shakir — A Woman's Voice

Maiñ ḳhayāloñ meñ tumhāre rāsteñ naaptī rahī
Tum mere qadmoñ tale āʾe to patā hī na chalā

Woh jo ham meñ tum meñ qarār thā tumheñ yād ho ki na yād ho
Woh jo hum pe qarz-e-mohabbat thā tumheñ yād ho ki na yād ho — Parveen Shakir
Parveen Shakir's Contribution: As one of the most prominent female ghazal poets, Parveen Shakir brought a distinctly feminine perspective to the ghazal tradition, which had been predominantly male for centuries. She used feminine verb forms and openly expressed desire from a woman's point of view — revolutionary in Urdu poetry.

9.7 Agha Shahid Ali — The English Ghazal

The only language of loss left in the world is Arabic —
These words were said to me in a language not Arabic.

Ancestors, you've left me a disinherited prince —
how do I translate your lives lived in Arabic? — Agha Shahid Ali, from "Arabic"
The English Ghazal: Agha Shahid Ali pioneered the "real ghazal in English", insisting that English ghazals follow the traditional formal rules — qafiya, radif, thematic independence of each sher, and takhallus in the maqta. He criticized the loose "ghazals" written by Western poets that abandoned the form's musical structure. His collection "Call Me Ishmael Tonight" remains the gold standard for English-language ghazals.

Hafiz Shirazi 1315–1390 Persian Supreme master of Persian ghazal; Sufi mysticism blended with worldly beauty
Rumi (Jalāl ad-Dīn) 1207–1273 Persian Spiritual ecstasy; whirling dervish tradition; universal themes of love and unity
Mir Taqi Mir 1723–1810 Urdu "Khuda-e-Sukhan" (God of Poetry); simplicity, raw emotion, melancholy
Mirza Ghalib 1797–1869 Urdu / Persian Intellectual depth, wordplay, philosophical complexity, wit
Allama Iqbal 1877–1938 Urdu / Persian Khudi (self-empowerment), spirituality, political philosophy
Faiz Ahmed Faiz 1911–1984 Urdu Progressive poetry; fusion of romantic and revolutionary themes
Ahmad Faraz 1931–2008 Urdu Romantic intensity; accessible modern language; iconic ghazals
Jaun Elia 1931–2002 Urdu Raw emotional expression; existential themes; modern imagery
Sahir Ludhianvi 1921–1980 Urdu Film lyrics; progressive themes; accessible romanticism
Parveen Shakir 1952–1994 Urdu Feminine voice; modern sensibility; romantic and feminist themes
Agha Shahid Ali 1949–2001 English / Urdu English-language ghazal; formal adherence; themes of exile and Kashmir
